How to write a consulting proposal or SOW with Claude Code

A proposal wins on clarity: what the client said, what you will do, what it costs and what is out of scope. This guide shows how to have Claude Code draft all of that as an editable Word file, without letting it invent facts or legal terms.

By Boardroom Skills · Updated 2026-11-09 · About 4 min read

Short version: a proposal that wins usually restates the client’s problem in their words, shows a phased plan with deliverables you can check, and offers three priced options. Claude Code can draft all of it as an editable .docx. You supply the facts and prices; legal terms stay as placeholders for a lawyer.

Proposal or SOW: what is the difference?

People use the words loosely. A proposal persuades: it shows you understand the problem and why your approach is right. A statement of work (SOW) specifies: scope, deliverables, schedule, assumptions and what is excluded. For small consulting engagements it is common, and sensible, to combine them into one document that does both. A SOW is also usually the thing attached to a contract, which is why the legal terms are a separate matter, covered below.

Step 1: Write the brief down first

Before you open Claude Code, gather these facts. Everything else is writing; these are the parts the AI cannot know.

  • Provider and client names, and the contact person at the client.
  • The problem in the client’s own words, ideally quoted from the discovery call or email.
  • The goal and how success would be measured, labelled as the client’s target, not your guarantee.
  • Scope: what you will do, and what you will not.
  • Timeline and start date.
  • Prices for each option. If you have not decided, stop and decide. A proposal with invented prices is a liability.

Step 2: Use a proven structure

  1. Executive summary. Problem, proposed solution, outcome and price range, in two short paragraphs plus a three-bullet “at a glance” box. Many readers stop here.
  2. Understanding of needs. A table of what you heard and what it means. State that you will validate it in the first phase.
  3. Approach. Three to five phases with weeks, three activities each and one milestone. A simple Gantt-style timeline helps the reader see the plan.
  4. Deliverables. Numbered, each checkable: what it is, its form, how acceptance works.
  5. Assumptions. Five to eight specific, testable statements: access to people and data, approval turnaround, what is excluded, travel.
  6. Pricing. Three options, Good, Better and Best, as fixed fees. Options are cumulative and the rows show exactly what each includes. Mark one as recommended and say why.
  7. Terms and signature. Payment schedule, validity date, and a signature block.

Three options work because they change the client’s question from “should we hire you?” to “which level do we want?”. Keep the price ascending and be honest about what the lowest option leaves out.

Step 3: Generate the draft

With a plain prompt, ask for the structure above and tell Claude Code to produce a Word file using python-docx with real heading styles (not bold text pretending to be headings), so the client can edit it and the navigation pane works. Or use the free proposal-sow skill:

git clone https://github.com/boardroomskills/boardroom-skills.git
cp -r boardroom-skills/skills/proposal-sow ~/.claude/skills/
pip install -r ~/.claude/skills/proposal-sow/requirements.txt

Then prompt:

Use the proposal-sow skill. Client brief is in brief.md. Ask me for
any missing prices, dates or client facts instead of inventing them.
Leave legal terms as bracketed placeholders. Build the .docx and PDF
and list every assumption I need to confirm.

The skill reads a JSON spec (an example is in skills/proposal-sow/examples/proposal.json) and builds the document with python scripts/build_proposal.py spec.json out_dir. The PDF is rendered from the Word file by LibreOffice, so you need soffice on your path for that part; --no-pdf skips it.

Step 4: Keep the legal terms out of the AI’s hands

Liability caps, intellectual property, confidentiality, termination and governing law are legal clauses. Ask the agent to insert bracketed placeholders such as [Limitation of liability - to be inserted] and nothing more, then paste in wording from your lawyer or your standard terms. Drafting these by AI and sending them unreviewed is the single riskiest shortcut in this whole process. This document is a commercial proposal, not a contract.

Step 5: Cross-check before it leaves

Most embarrassing proposals have internal contradictions. Check these yourself:

  • Option prices in the pricing table match the executive summary and the payment schedule.
  • Total weeks match the timeline, and phase names are spelled the same everywhere.
  • Each deliverable reference (D1, D2…) appears in the pricing table against the right option.
  • No placeholders remain except the intentional bracketed terms. A quick pdftotext proposal.pdf - | grep -n "TBD\|TODO\|\[" finds them.
  • Client outcomes are phrased as the client’s targets, not promises from you.
  • Names and company details are spelled exactly as the client writes them.

Also look at the PDF pages for a heading stranded at the bottom of a page, a table split awkwardly, or a signature block that runs over two pages.

Step 6: Adapt it to your voice

AI drafts tend towards smooth, generic language. Replace phrases such as “holistic” and “best-in-class” with specifics: which systems, how many interviews, which report the client will receive on which date. Short sentences. Numbers instead of adjectives. And read the executive summary aloud; if you would be embarrassed to say it to the client, rewrite it.

What the draft will not do for you

  • Decide your price. The tool uses the numbers you give it.
  • Know the client. Discovery work is yours.
  • Replace your lawyer on terms.
  • Match your firm’s brand. The default is a single typeface with navy and teal; changing colours is a theme file, deeper branding means editing the template or finishing in Word.
